The best interests duty in plain English: the steps, the related obligations and the proof

The duty is one sentence in the Corporations Act. Showing you met it takes a whole file. Here’s how the pieces fit.

The short answer

What is the best interests duty?

The best interests duty in section 961B of the Corporations Act requires anyone giving personal advice to a retail client to act in the client’s best interests in relation to that advice. The seven safe harbour steps in s961B(2) are one way to prove it. Related obligations require appropriate advice, warnings about incomplete information and priority to the client’s interests.

Key takeaways
  • The safe harbour has seven steps. If you can’t show every one of them on the file, you can’t rely on it.
  • Disclosing a conflict, or getting the client’s consent to it, doesn’t meet the duty to prioritise the client’s interests.
  • Licensees must keep records of the information relied on and the steps taken, for at least seven years after the advice.

What is the best interests duty?

A licensee file review lands in your inbox with one question beside an SOA you wrote in March: ‘Where does the file show you checked the client’s existing insurance before recommending the rollover?’ You remember the conversation clearly. The file doesn’t mention it.

That gap is what the best interests duty is about in practice. Section 961B(1) of the Corporations Act says the provider ‘must act in the best interests of the client in relation to the advice’. It applies to personal advice given to retail clients, not to general advice.

ASIC’s RG 175 explains how it assesses the duty. It asks whether a reasonable advice provider would believe the client is likely to be in a better position if they follow the advice, judged on the facts at the time rather than with hindsight (RG 175.164 to RG 175.165). ASIC doesn’t expect perfect advice, and the duty doesn’t stop you charging fees. If you can’t act in the client’s best interests, you must not give the advice (RG 175.176).

What are the safe harbour steps in s961B(2)?

Section 961B(2) says the provider satisfies the duty if they prove they have done each of the following. In plain English:

  • (a) Identified the client’s objectives, financial situation and needs, as disclosed through their instructions.
  • (b) Identified the subject matter of the advice sought, whether stated or implied, and the circumstances relevant to it (the client’s ‘relevant circumstances’).
  • (c) Where it was reasonably apparent that information was incomplete or inaccurate, made reasonable inquiries to get complete and accurate information.
  • (d) Assessed whether they have the expertise to advise on that subject matter and, if not, declined to give the advice.
  • (e) If it’s reasonable to consider recommending a product, reasonably investigated products that might meet the relevant objectives and needs, and assessed the findings.
  • (f) Based all judgements on the client’s relevant circumstances.
  • (g) Taken any other step that, at the time, would reasonably be regarded as in the client’s best interests, given their relevant circumstances.

The safe harbour is one way to satisfy the duty, not the only way, but ASIC expects these steps, or others giving at least the same standard of advice, whenever you give personal advice (RG 175.178 to RG 175.179). If you can’t show every element, you can’t rely on the safe harbour (RG 175.188). And a reasonable investigation doesn’t mean every product available, but if the client asks you to consider a particular product, it includes that one (s961D).

A modified duty, needing only steps (a) to (c), applies to general insurance advice and to some bank advice on basic banking products.

What are the related obligations?

The duty sits in Division 2 of Part 7.7A beside three related obligations. ASIC calls the four together the ‘best interests duty and related obligations’.

ObligationWhat it requiresIn practice
Appropriate advice (s961G)Only give advice if it would be reasonable to conclude it’s appropriate, assuming you met the best interests duty. ASIC reads this as fit for purpose and likely to leave the client better off (RG 175.282).The reasons in the SOA tie each recommendation to the client’s goals and circumstances.
Warning on incomplete or inaccurate information (s961H)If it’s reasonably apparent that the information the advice relies on is incomplete or inaccurate, warn the client, at the same time and by the same means as the advice, to consider whether it’s appropriate before acting. It can go in the SOA.A warning doesn’t replace reasonable inquiries (RG 175.306). ASIC suggests saying which information is incomplete.
Priority to the client’s interests (s961J)If you know, or reasonably ought to know, of a conflict between the client’s interests and yours, your licensee’s or your associates’, give priority to the client’s interests.Disclosure or consent alone doesn’t comply (RG 175.311). Ask what a reasonable adviser without the conflict would do (RG 175.315).

Licensees must also take reasonable steps to make sure their representatives comply with all four (s961L).

Can you scale advice and still meet the duty?

Yes. ASIC says all personal advice can be scaled, and there aren’t two sets of rules, one for comprehensive advice and one for limited advice (RG 175.329 to RG 175.331). The note to s961B(2) itself anticipates scaled advice.

ASIC’s INFO 267, Tips for giving limited advice, sets out how to do it well:

  • Ask the questions needed to identify relevant circumstances, even on topics outside the scope.
  • Verify where you need to, for example by checking super statements, rather than relying only on what the client says.
  • Don’t narrow the scope to leave out a critical issue, or because a topic sits outside your usual advice model.
  • Make sure the client understands significant limitations, and record the scope, your reasons for it and its implications in the SOA.

How does your workflow show the steps were taken?

The duty is met in the work, but it’s shown in the file. ASIC warns that processes producing one-size-fits-all outcomes are unlikely to be effective (RG 175.174). Here’s how the usual stages of advice map to the steps, and what the file should hold:

StageStepsEvidence on the file
Discovery meeting(a), (b)An approved file note: the client’s instructions, goals in their own words, the subject matter and the agreed scope.
Fact find(b), (c)Relevant circumstances with the source of each answer, gaps raised as questions, and statements checked.
Scope and expertise(d)A note that the adviser can advise on the subject matter, or the referral if not.
Strategy and research(e), (f)Strategies and products considered, why options were rejected, and the adviser’s assumptions.
Statement of advice(f), (g), s961G, s961H, s961JReasons linked to the client’s circumstances, alternatives, risks, any warning, conflicts and fees.
Approval and filingAllWho checked and approved what, and when; the signed authority; everything kept for seven years.

The fact find is where many files are won or lost. If an answer has no source, or a gap was filled with an assumption, step (c) is hard to prove. A fact find that shows where each answer came from, kept with the approved meeting file note, makes it far easier. The SOA then explains the result: see our guide to the statement of advice.

What records show you met the best interests duty?

Under notional section 912G, inserted by ASIC Instrument 2024/508, licensees must ensure records are kept of (RG 175.341):

  • the information relied on and action taken that show the provider acted in the client’s best interests
  • if relying on the safe harbour, the information relied on and action taken for each step in s961B(2)
  • the advice given, including why it was reasonable to conclude it was appropriate
  • where there was a conflict, the information relied on and action taken to give the client’s interests priority

The records must be kept for at least seven years after the advice is given, in a way the licensee can produce them (RG 175.342). They don’t have to be paper: ASIC’s examples include the advice document, file notes and records of conversations, correspondence, working papers, fact-finding documents and audio recordings (RG 175.337). Some of these requirements don’t apply where no SOA is required (RG 175.347).

A file check you can run today. Pick a recent file and ask a colleague who wasn’t involved to find:

  • the client’s instructions and goals, in their own words
  • the scope, and why it was set that way
  • the source of each fact find answer, and the inquiries made about gaps
  • the strategies and products considered, and why options were rejected
  • reasons linking each recommendation to the client’s circumstances
  • any incomplete-information warning, and how conflicts were handled
  • who approved the advice, and when

The same test underpins a wider advice file review.

What does this look like on a client file?

Worked example (fictional)

Priya and Arjun Shah ask Mara Holloway at Aynub Financial for advice on retirement and super. They plan to retire at 60 and 63. Arjun also asks Mara to look at a managed fund a colleague mentioned.

Mara’s approved file note records their goals in their own words and the agreed scope. They’d like to leave insurance out, but the fact find shows Arjun’s only life cover is inside his super, so any change to his super affects it. Mara keeps that cover in scope and explains why.

Priya’s super balance in the fact find comes from last year’s statement. Mara asks for the current one before any modelling. Arjun’s hours may drop next year, so she models both cases and records the assumption she used.

She includes the fund Arjun named in her research, as s961D requires, and notes why she isn’t recommending it. The SOA links each recommendation to the Shahs’ goals, and the file holds the note, the sourced fact find, the research and Mara’s approval with its date.

When the licensee’s reviewer asks how the steps were met, the answer is in the file, not in Mara’s memory.

Is the best interests duty changing?

On 19 August 2026 the Assistant Treasurer said the government will ‘deliver targeted reforms to the best interest duty to enable the provision of scaled advice’, without giving a timeline. Until any change is legislated and in force, the current law applies. When we checked on 11 November 2026, section 961B and its safe harbour were unchanged in the Corporations Act compilation in force from 19 September 2026.

Common questions

Does the best interests duty apply to general advice?

No. The best interests duty and related obligations apply to personal advice given to retail clients. General advice has its own obligations, including a general advice warning.

Who owes the best interests duty, the adviser or the licensee?

The duty sits with the advice provider, generally the individual who gives the advice. Licensees must take reasonable steps to ensure their representatives comply, and a responsible licensee can contravene a civil penalty provision when a provider fails to comply.

Can a client agree to waive the best interests duty?

No. A contract term that tries to waive the obligations is void, and ASIC says notices and disclaimers can’t be used to avoid them. Being clear about the scope of the advice isn’t the same as contracting out.

Is a warning enough if the client won’t provide information?

No. The s961H warning doesn’t remove the obligation to make reasonable inquiries, act in the client’s best interests and give appropriate advice. If you can’t do those, ASIC says you must not give the advice.

Does the best interests duty mean recommending the cheapest option?

Not necessarily. ASIC says features a client particularly values can be relevant, if the client understands and is prepared to pay for them. But in one of its examples, moving a cost-conscious client to a dearer platform for easier reports failed the duty.
