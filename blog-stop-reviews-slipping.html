A Client Review Process for Financial Advisers That Stops Reviews Slipping

Reviews rarely slip because someone forgot. They slip at the handovers nobody owns. Here’s how to close them.

The short answer

How do financial advisers stop client reviews slipping?

Give every review a reliable start date, one named owner and a visible next action. A good client review process for financial advisers starts preparation from the client’s real dates, tracks each stage from invitation to follow-up, keeps any fee consent renewal in step with the review, and checks the queue weekly, so stuck work shows up early.

Key takeaways
  • Start each review from the client’s real dates, with preparation time built in, not from someone noticing.
  • Every review needs one accountable owner and a next action with a date, even when several people help.
  • Check the queue weekly by waiting reason, then fix the handover that keeps causing the delay.

Why do client reviews slip?

It’s the first Monday in March. The practice manager filters the review spreadsheet and finds four households whose reviews were due before Christmas. One invitation went out and nobody chased the reply. One meeting was postponed and the calendar entry was simply deleted. Two never started, because the person who usually notices was on leave in January.

Nobody was careless. The client review process for financial advisers in most small firms works only while the right person remembers. It breaks at the handovers: a review sits on a calendar with no one assigned to prepare it, an invitation goes out with no one tracking the response, or the meeting is held while its actions stay in the adviser’s notebook.

Before you change anything, follow three recent reviews through what actually happened. Mark each step that had a clear owner, and each step that depended on someone noticing a gap. The second list is where your reviews slip.

What should start each review, and who owns it?

Agree what starts each review cycle. For most firms it’s a date already on the client record: the anniversary of the last review, of the advice, or of the service agreement. Then agree how much preparation time the team needs before the meeting, and work back from it. Check that the source dates are complete and correct, because a review process built on missing dates fails quietly.

When a cycle starts, it gets one accountable owner and a clear next step. Several people may help, but one name answers for whether the review is moving. If a client can’t be reached or a date changes, record the exception and assign the follow-up rather than leaving the review in limbo.

Fee renewals have their own clock

If the client has an ongoing fee arrangement, consent to renew it has its own timing rules under the Corporations Act, explained in ASIC’s INFO 286. It often makes sense to prepare the renewal with the review, but track its date separately, because moving a meeting doesn’t move the consent date. See how to renew an ongoing fee arrangement.

What stages should a client review workflow have?

Use stages that describe real progress, and agree what has to be true before work moves on. A simple sequence works for most firms:

StageReady to move on when
DueThe cycle has started, an owner is named and the preparation date is set.
InviteThe invitation has gone and the reply is tracked: the meeting is booked or the exception is recorded.
PrepareWhat’s changed since the last review is gathered with its sources, and the adviser has checked the pack.
MeetThe meeting is held and the file note is approved.
Follow upEvery action has an owner and a due date, and the follow-up email has gone.
CompleteActions are done or deliberately carried forward, and a colleague can find the record.

The important line is between Meet and Complete. Holding the meeting doesn’t close the review. Many reviews that look finished are still waiting on a form, a document or a call nobody owns.

What should a review queue show?

A calendar shows when meetings happen. A queue shows whether the work around them is moving. Keep it focused on what helps the team decide what needs attention today:

InformationWhy it matters
Client and review periodIdentifies the household and the work being managed.
Due date and stageShows timing alongside actual progress.
OwnerMakes accountability explicit, even when several people contribute.
Next action and check dateTurns a status into something someone can act on.
Waiting reasonSeparates a missing document from a client who hasn’t replied or an internal handover.
Fee consent dateShows when any renewal is due, so a late review doesn’t become a late consent.

Detail can stay on the client record. The queue’s job is to show what’s stuck and why.

What happens when a review meeting moves?

Worked example (fictional)

Aynub Financial is a fee-for-service practice in Kingaroy. Three weeks before a retired couple’s review, they ask to push it back a month for a family trip. Under the old process, the calendar entry would have been deleted and the review would have reappeared in March.

Instead, Aynub’s client services officer records the postponement against the review, with the reason, and stays its owner. The next action is to offer new times when the couple are home, with a check date in the queue. Adviser Mara Holloway looks at the fee consent date: the renewal is still due on its own date, so it goes ahead on time rather than waiting for the meeting.

When the meeting is rebooked, the pack is checked again, because a new super statement has arrived. After the meeting, three actions are assigned with owners and dates. The review is marked complete only when the last one is done.

The improvement isn’t speed. It’s that anyone at Aynub can see why the review is waiting, who owns it and what happens next.

A weekly review checklist you can use today

Run this as a fifteen-minute team check, adapted to your firm’s cadence:

  • Does every active review have one named owner?
  • Which reviews are overdue, or inside their preparation window with nothing started?
  • Which items have no next action or no check date?
  • Which invitations, client replies or documents are still outstanding?
  • Have postponed meetings kept their review record and owner?
  • Are any fee consent renewals due before their review meeting?
  • Which completed meetings are still waiting on actions or approval?
  • Could a colleague find the supporting record for work marked complete?

Look for repeated causes. If several reviews wait at the same handover, fix that step instead of adding another reminder to each file.

Where does AI help with review preparation?

With agreed inputs and permissions, AI can do the gathering that eats preparation time: what’s changed since the last review, drawn from emails, documents and meetings, with the source of each item. It can draft the invitation, the review pack and, after the meeting, the file note and follow-up with Meeting Notes. Our guide to AI meeting notes for advisers covers that last step.

A person still checks the sources, the omissions and the proposed actions. Keep preparation and completion separate: a well-prepared pack doesn’t show that the review happened, that actions were completed or that obligations were met.

Your next step: this week, pick the three most recent reviews that ran late. Trace each one back to the handover where it stalled, agree an owner for that handover, and add it to your weekly check.

Common questions

Is a calendar reminder enough to manage client reviews?

A reminder can prompt action, but it doesn’t say who owns the preparation, what’s outstanding or whether the follow-up is done. Connect each reminder to a review with an owner, a stage and a next action.

Should every client follow the same review process?

A shared starting process helps, but different service levels may need different steps. Define the variations and the exception rules up front, rather than leaving each person to improvise.

Who should own a client review: the adviser or the support team?

Often the support team owns the process and the adviser owns the advice decisions. What matters is that one name answers for whether each review is moving, and that everyone knows whose it is.

How far ahead should review preparation start?

Far enough ahead to book the meeting, gather documents and hear back from the client. Set the lead time by service level, then compare it with how long preparation actually takes and adjust.

Should the fee consent renewal happen at the review meeting?

Preparing the renewal with the review is often convenient, but the consent has its own timing rules. If a meeting moves, check the renewal date separately, using ASIC’s guidance and your licensee’s process.

What should we measure first?

Start with overdue reviews, reviews without an owner, items with no next action and time spent waiting at handovers. Compare against your own baseline rather than a universal target.
