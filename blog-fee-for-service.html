The short answer

What do you need for an ongoing fee arrangement renewal?

Ongoing fee arrangement renewal needs the client’s written consent, signed and dated, after you’ve disclosed in writing the services, fees and period it covers. ASIC’s guidance sets a window around the arrangement’s anniversary, and the arrangement terminates if consent isn’t given. Keep the consent alongside records of the service you actually delivered.

Key takeaways
  • Renewal consent must be written, signed and dated, after a written disclosure of the services, fees and period it covers.
  • ASIC’s consent window runs from 60 days before to 150 days after the anniversary, or an agreed reference day.
  • The consent shows what was agreed; notes, tasks and documents show what was delivered. Keep them linked.

What does ongoing fee arrangement renewal involve?

A client rings in May. ‘I sign something about fees every year. What am I actually getting for it?’ The adviser knows the answer: two reviews, a mid-year call, help with a super rollover. But can anyone in the office show it in two minutes, from the file, with the consent beside it?

That’s the real work behind ongoing fee arrangement renewal. Under the Corporations Act, an ongoing fee arrangement exists when you give personal advice to a retail client and, under the arrangement, the client pays a fee over a period of more than 12 months. Changes made by the Delivering Better Financial Outcomes legislation took effect from 10 January 2025, and ASIC’s INFO 286 explains how they work. It applies to arrangements entered into from that date, and to earlier arrangements from their first anniversary after it.

Two things have to line up each year: the client’s consent to keep paying, and evidence of the service they received. This article covers both, from an operational point of view.

What must the client be told before they consent?

According to INFO 286, before the client consents to enter into or renew the arrangement, you must tell them in writing:

  • the fee recipient’s name and contact details
  • why their consent is being sought
  • the longest period the consent could cover before it stops having effect
  • the services they’ll receive in that period
  • each fee amount, or a reasonable estimate and how it’s worked out, and how often it’s charged
  • that they can end the arrangement at any time
  • that the arrangement will terminate if they don’t consent, and when

The consent itself must be in writing, signed and dated by the client, and kept by the fee recipient. ASIC says it can be given electronically but not verbally, and there’s currently no prescribed form. Deducting fees from a client’s account needs its own written consent, naming the account holders and the account and setting out the amounts. The two can be combined in one form, as long as that form meets every requirement for each.

A summary, not the law

This is a plain-English summary of ASIC’s guidance as it stood when we checked it. Read INFO 286 in full and confirm your forms and process with your licensee.

When does the renewal window open and close?

ASIC’s guidance sets a window for getting renewal consent. It opens 60 days before the anniversary of the day the arrangement was entered into, and closes 150 days after it. The client and fee recipient can instead agree a reference day, and the same window then runs around that day. INFO 286 sets out the conditions.

If consent isn’t given, the arrangement terminates. No further fees can be charged under it, and there’s no obligation to keep providing the services.

In practice, the date that matters is the one the consent was signed, not the date of the next review. Track each arrangement’s anniversary and window on the client record, and start the renewal when the window opens. If you link renewals to your client review process, watch for reviews that move: the consent date doesn’t move with them.

Why isn’t a signed consent enough on its own?

A consent records what was agreed: the services, the fees and the period. Meeting notes, completed tasks and documents record what happened. Treat either as a stand-in for the other and the history becomes hard to follow.

INFO 286 also lists records to keep for at least five years, including each consent, the notifications given to the client, the information disclosed, and the termination date and its basis. Beyond that, your service record should let someone else answer these questions:

RecordQuestion it helps answer
Consent and disclosureWhat services and fees did the client agree to, for which period, and when did they sign?
ActivityWhat work was done, when and by whom?
Source materialWhich notes, documents or correspondence support the account of the work?
Review decisionWho checked the output, and what did they decide?
ExceptionWhat’s missing, changed or unfinished, and who is managing it?
AccessCan the right people find all of this without relying on one person’s inbox?

A field being filled in isn’t proof its contents are right. Check the substance of the record, not just its presence.

What does a clean renewal look like?

Worked example (fictional)

Priya and Arjun Shah are planning to retire at 60 and 63. A year after their advice was put in place, their adviser’s team sees the arrangement’s anniversary coming up on the Shahs’ record, and the renewal window opening 60 days before it.

The renewal is prepared with their annual review. The disclosure lists the services for the coming period, the fee and how often it’s charged, that they can end the arrangement at any time, and when it would terminate without their consent. A person checks it before it goes. Priya and Arjun sign and date the consent electronically, and it’s filed on their record with the date signed. Next year’s due date is tracked from it.

At the review meeting, one action stays open: Arjun’s updated super statement hasn’t arrived. The meeting is recorded as held, and the action stays open with an owner and a check date, rather than hiding under a single ‘complete’.

When Arjun rings in May to ask what the fee covers, anyone on the team can open the record and show him: the consent, the services it listed, and the meetings and work delivered since.

How do you test whether a renewal file holds up?

Pick a file and ask an authorised colleague who wasn’t involved to follow it. Can they:

  • find the signed, dated consent and the written disclosure that came before it?
  • see that it was given inside the window, and when the next one is due?
  • match any account deduction consent to the right accounts and amounts?
  • tell planned services apart from work actually done?
  • follow dates, owners and documents as one consistent story?
  • see outstanding actions, each with an owner and a next step?
  • reach every source without relying on one person’s inbox or memory?

Route any discrepancy to the right person. Missing evidence should trigger an investigation. It should never be filled with assumptions, or with notes written later and presented as if they were made at the time. The same test underpins a wider advice file review.

Where can AI help with fee records, and where can’t it?

AI can track dates, prepare the renewal paperwork for a person to check, and pull together the meetings and work delivered in the period, with links to the sources. Tools like Meeting Notes mean the record of each meeting exists from the day it happens.

What AI can’t do is establish that a service was delivered. A summary is only as good as the records under it, and a draft is a draft until an authorised person has checked it.

Your next step: this month, list every ongoing fee arrangement with its anniversary and window dates beside the next review date. Anywhere the renewal is due before the review, decide now how the team will handle it.

Common questions

Does a signed consent prove the service was delivered?

No. Consent and service activity answer different questions. Keep the consent with the records of what actually happened, and have the responsible people assess any obligations.

Can a client give fee consent verbally?

ASIC’s guidance says no. Consent must be in writing, signed and dated by the client, though it can be given electronically.

Can the renewal and account deduction consents be on one form?

ASIC’s guidance says the information can be combined into a single form, as long as that form meets all the requirements for each consent. Check the combined form with your licensee.

What happens if a client doesn’t renew their ongoing fee arrangement?

The arrangement terminates, no further fees can be charged under it, and there’s no obligation to keep providing its services. Record the termination date and its basis, which ASIC lists among the records to keep.

How long should fee consent records be kept?

ASIC’s INFO 286 lists records to keep for at least five years, including consents, client notifications and the information disclosed. Your licensee may set further requirements.

Can software guarantee fee consent compliance?

No. Software can track dates, keep records together and route work for approval. Your firm still defines its process, checks the work and stays responsible for its obligations.
