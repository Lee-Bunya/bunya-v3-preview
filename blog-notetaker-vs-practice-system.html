AI notetaker for financial advisers, or a whole practice system?

A good note tool gives an adviser back the write-up. Whether that’s enough depends on what happens after the note.

The short answer

Is an AI notetaker enough for a financial advice firm?

An AI notetaker for financial advisers is enough when writing file notes is the main pain and your current system already handles tasks, reviews, fee consent and approvals well. If work stalls after the note, details are retyped into several systems, or you can’t show who approved what, the gap is the workflow, and a practice system fits better.

Key takeaways
  • A note tool fixes the write-up. It doesn’t move the actions, the email, the fact find or the next stage.
  • Count how often your team retypes what was said in a meeting. That number tells you which tool you need.
  • Either way, record the client’s consent and keep who approved the note, and when, on the client’s file.

What does an AI notetaker actually do for an adviser?

The meeting with the Shahs ends at 4:00. By 4:05 a tidy summary is in the adviser’s inbox: what Priya and Arjun want, the figures they mentioned, a list of next steps. It’s a genuine improvement on typing it up at 8pm.

An AI notetaker for financial advisers records a meeting with the client’s consent, transcribes it and drafts a file note, often with a list of actions. The better ones use advice-specific templates, so the note reads like a file note rather than a chat summary. Some connect to a CRM and attach the note to the client.

Then the note sits there. Someone still has to turn the actions into tasks, write the follow-up email, update the fact find, move the household to the next stage and book the review. That work is where most of the hours go.

Where does the work go after the note?

List everything that should happen after one client meeting, and who does it today:

  1. The file note is checked, corrected and filed with the client.
  2. Each action gets a named owner and a due date.
  3. The follow-up email goes to the client, in the firm’s voice.
  4. New details (a changed income, a new goal, a house move) reach the fact find and the client record.
  5. The household moves to its next stage, and the right person sees it’s their turn.
  6. The review date and any fee consent due are set.
  7. Who checked and approved each piece is recorded.

A note tool covers the first item well, and sometimes the second. A practice system is built to carry all seven, because the note, the record and the next step live in the same place.

When is an AI note tool enough?

A note tool is often the right call. It’s quick to start, it’s usually priced per adviser, and it doesn’t ask your team to change how they work. It’s enough when:

  • Writing file notes is the bottleneck, and everything after the note already runs well.
  • Your CRM holds tasks, reviews and fee consent reliably, and the note tool connects to it properly, not just as an attachment.
  • You have one or two advisers, and the person who writes the note also does the follow-up.
  • You’re not planning to change your core system in the next year or two.

When does a firm need a practice system instead?

The signs are usually visible within a week of looking for them:

  • Retyping. What the client said ends up typed again into the CRM, the fact find and the advice documents.
  • Lost actions. Next steps live in a note nobody reopens, so follow-up depends on memory.
  • Stalled households. Clients wait between steps because nobody can see whose turn it is.
  • Slipping reviews and consents. Reviews start when someone notices, not from real dates. Stopping reviews slipping covers this in detail.
  • No trail. You can’t easily show who checked an AI draft, what they changed or when they approved it.
  • Too many tools. A note tool, a CRM, spreadsheets, e-signing and a portal, each with its own login and bill.

If three or more of these sound familiar, the problem isn’t the note. It’s the workflow, and a better note won’t fix it. Why one system beats a patchwork explains the wider shift.

Worked example: one Tuesday, two ways

Worked example (fictional)

Priya and Arjun Shah meet their adviser at 4:00 to talk about retiring at 60 and 63. Every name and detail is made up for this example.

With a note tool. The draft note arrives at 4:05. The adviser goes straight into the next meeting. At 8:00 that night she checks the note, copies the actions into the CRM, updates the fact find with the Shahs’ new figures and starts the follow-up email from a blank page. It goes out at 9:10. The household’s stage is updated the next morning, when she remembers.

With a practice system. At 4:02 the file note, the actions with owners and a draft email are waiting in the Shahs’ record, with the new figures flagged for the fact find. Between meetings she checks them, changes one line and approves. The email is with the Shahs at 4:07, the household has moved to fact find, and her paraplanner can see it’s their turn.

The note was equally good both ways. The difference was everything after it.

What should you check about consent and AI governance either way?

Whichever type you choose, the same questions apply, and your licensee will likely ask them:

  • Consent. How is the client told the meeting is recorded, and where is their agreement kept? Meetings away from the office need the same care as video calls.
  • Where data goes. Where are recordings, transcripts and notes stored, and where does the AI processing happen? The OAIC’s guidance on commercially available AI products is a good checklist.
  • Human review. Who checks each AI draft before it’s filed or sent, and is that recorded with the date?
  • Policy. Does your firm, or your licensee, have a written policy on AI use and on telling clients about it?

That last point is often missing. When ASIC reviewed 23 licensees’ use of AI, only 10 had policies that referenced disclosure of AI use to affected consumers (ASIC Report 798, October 2024). For a step-by-step workflow, see AI meeting notes for financial advisers.

How do you test the difference before you commit?

Run the same test on any note tool and any practice system you’re considering, using example or de-identified data:

  1. Record one mock client meeting with a colleague playing the client.
  2. Time how long until the note is drafted, checked and filed with the client.
  3. Keep the clock running until every action has an owner, the client email has gone and the next stage is set.
  4. Count every time someone types something that was already said in the meeting.
  5. Ask to see where the approval is recorded, and by whom.
Measure the whole job

The time that matters isn’t how fast the note appears. It’s how long until the work the meeting created is done and recorded.

Common questions

What is an AI notetaker for financial advisers?

It’s software that records a client meeting with consent, transcribes it and drafts a file note, often with a list of actions. Some use advice-specific templates and connect to a CRM. A person still needs to check the note before it’s filed.

Can we use Microsoft Copilot as our notetaker?

Many firms start there because it’s already in Microsoft 365. Check that the summary meets your file-note standard, that a person reviews it, and how actions and follow-up reach your client record. A meeting summary isn’t automatically a compliant file note.

Is a transcript the same as a file note?

No. A transcript records what was said. A file note records what matters for the advice: the client’s circumstances, what was discussed, what was agreed and what happens next, checked by the adviser.

Do note tools work for meetings in person?

Some do, usually through a phone app, and some only work with video calls. Check how consent is captured and where the recording is stored before you rely on it for meetings away from the office.

Can we start with a note tool and move to a practice system later?

Yes. Just check what you can export from the note tool, including notes, transcripts and approval history, so the record moves with you.

Who is responsible if an AI note is wrong?

Your firm. AI can draft the note, but the adviser is responsible for checking it, so build the review into the workflow and record who approved it.
