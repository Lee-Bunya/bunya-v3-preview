The short answer

How do you work out the true cost of financial advice software?

To find the real financial advice software cost, compare every option on the same scope and period, often three years. Add setup, migration, recurring licences, AI and usage charges, platform licences, tools you’ll still pay for, support and team time. Treat a missing quote as unknown, not zero, and keep possible savings out of the total.

Key takeaways
  • A cheaper subscription can cost more once you add the separate tools it doesn’t replace.
  • Check how each supplier counts: per seat, per client household, per module or by usage.
  • Put team time and AI usage in the worksheet, but keep hoped-for savings out of the total.

Why is the licence price a poor guide to financial advice software cost?

It’s the end of the financial year and you’re going through the direct debits. The CRM. The e-signing tool. The modelling licence. The client portal. The call recording service. Then there’s the spreadsheet that tracks every SOA in progress, which only one person really understands. None of it looks expensive on its own.

That’s the problem with comparing financial advice software cost on the monthly fee. Two quotes can look $500 a month apart and cover completely different work. One replaces three of those tools. The other replaces one and leaves you paying for the rest, and leaves your team copying information between them.

A fair comparison asks one question of every option: what will it cost us, in cash and in time, to run the same work over the same period?

How do you compare quotes that cover different things?

Start with a list of what the firm actually needs, before you look at any price. Include client records, the advice pipeline, meeting notes, reviews and fee consent, document handling, reporting, and any fact find, modelling or SOA work. Add calling, client portal and marketing only if they’re part of this decision.

Then mark every requirement against every quote as one of four things:

  • Included in the price quoted.
  • Priced separately by the same supplier.
  • Kept elsewhere, in a tool you’ll keep paying for.
  • Unconfirmed, which is an unknown cost, not a zero.

Use the same period for every option. Three years is a useful planning horizon because it covers setup, a renewal and some growth, but match it to the contract terms you’re actually being offered.

Per seat, per household or per module: which pricing model costs less?

None of them is cheaper in general. Each one grows with a different part of your firm, so test each against your own three-year plan.

  • Per seat or per user: the cost rises when you hire. Adding a paraplanner or a client services officer adds a licence, even if client numbers stay flat.
  • Per client or per household: the cost rises with your client book, not your headcount. Ask how a couple is counted, and whether their trusts, SMSFs and companies count separately.
  • Per module: you pay for the parts you switch on. Check which modules depend on others, and what the price becomes once you have the ones you need.
  • Usage-based: AI processing, storage or messages are charged as you use them. Ask for the charging unit and the assumption behind any estimate.
  • Flat firm fees: some add-ons are a fixed monthly price for the firm, whatever its size.

Model two scenarios: you hire two people, and you add 100 client households. The model that suits a growing, lean firm can look very different from one that suits a firm adding staff.

What belongs in a total cost worksheet?

Make a column for each option, including keeping what you have now. Record each amount and where it came from, and keep supplier quotes separate from your own estimates.

How should you budget for AI usage and growth?

For anything metered, ask the supplier to explain the charging unit and the usage behind their estimate. Then build a normal year and a busy year: more client households, longer documents, more meetings recorded, more storage.

Don’t assume your existing Microsoft 365 subscription covers every licence, integration or AI service a proposal needs. Ask which licences are extra, who bills them and roughly what they come to. If something is already paid for under an existing agreement, count only the extra cost this decision creates.

Should time savings count as a cost saving?

Keep them in a separate section. Optimistic savings shouldn’t hide a known commitment. Split them into time released, extra revenue and real reductions in spending, such as a tool you can cancel.

A measured saving of 15 minutes on 20 tasks a week releases five hours. It doesn’t reduce payroll or create five billable hours on its own. The team needs a use for that time, and the saving has to hold once checking and corrections are included. What should you ask every supplier before choosing?

Use this list on every quote, including the option of staying where you are:

  1. Are the same people, capabilities and support covered?
  2. Which prices are confirmed, estimated or missing?
  3. How is the price counted, and what happens when we hire or add households?
  4. Which of our current tools does it replace, and which do we keep paying for?
  5. What licences and usage are billed separately, and by whom?
  6. How much of our team’s time will setup, migration and training need?
  7. What does support include, and how are changes charged?
  8. What happens at renewal, and how do we get our data out if we leave?

Common questions

How much does financial advice software cost in Australia?

There’s no single figure, because suppliers price by seat, by client household, by module or by usage. Get quotes for your actual users, households and capabilities, then add migration, platform licences, the tools you’ll keep and your team’s time.

Is per-household pricing cheaper than per-seat pricing?

It depends on how your firm grows. Per-seat pricing rises when you hire, while per-household pricing rises with your client book. Model both against your three-year plan, and check how couples, trusts and SMSFs are counted.

Is a one-off build cheaper than a subscription?

Not automatically. A build still needs licences, hosting, maintenance and support. Compare complete costs over the same period rather than a build fee against one month of subscription.

Should staff time count as a software cost?

Yes, but show it separately from cash. Training, testing and data checks take time away from client work even when the staff are already employed.

Does our Microsoft 365 subscription cover software built on Microsoft?

Not necessarily. Some software needs additional Microsoft licences or metered AI services. Ask each supplier to list the licences it needs and who bills them.

Can we treat AI time savings as guaranteed?

No. Measure the whole workflow during a pilot, including checking and corrections, and confirm the saving holds in everyday use before you count on it.
