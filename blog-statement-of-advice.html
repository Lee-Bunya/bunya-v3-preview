Statement of advice: when you need one, what goes in it, and how to keep it short

An SOA has one job: helping the client decide whether to act on your advice. Here’s what the law requires, and what it doesn’t.

The short answer

What is a statement of advice?

A statement of advice (SOA) is the document an Australian adviser must generally give a retail client when they give personal advice. It sets out the advice, the basis for it, remuneration and other benefits, conflicts, and any warning about incomplete information. It must be clear, concise and effective, and kept for at least seven years.

Key takeaways
  • An SOA is required for personal advice to a retail client; further advice on unchanged circumstances can often be a record of advice instead.
  • ASIC expects a short, simple SOA for short, simple advice, with the key information in the body rather than an appendix.
  • The SOA explains the advice; the client file shows how you reached it. Keep the alternatives you rejected and your working papers on file.

What is a statement of advice?

Jo Reid rings the office two days after the strategy meeting. ‘The document arrived. It’s long. Do we really have to read all of it before we sign?’ If the honest answer is ‘not really’, the statement of advice isn’t doing its job.

A statement of advice (SOA) is the document a retail client receives when they get personal advice: advice where the adviser has considered one or more of their objectives, financial situation and needs, or a reasonable person might expect them to have. Under section 946A of the Corporations Act, the providing entity (the AFS licensee or authorised representative giving the advice) must give the client an SOA, subject to some exemptions.

ASIC’s Regulatory Guide 175 puts its purpose simply: an SOA helps a retail client understand, and decide whether to rely on, personal advice. The conduct obligations, including the best interests duty, decide what the advice is. The SOA documents it.

When is an SOA required, and when is a record of advice enough?

Generally, the SOA must be given at the same time as the advice or as soon as practicable after it, and in any event before you provide a further service connected with the advice, such as arranging for a product to be issued (s946C(1)). In time-critical cases, where the client expressly tells you to act straight away, the SOA can follow within five days, but some information must still be given when the advice is.

Fact-finding meetings don’t need their own SOA if they’re genuinely for gathering information and the client is told at the start that the advice will follow in an SOA (RG 175.91).

The exemptions an advice practice meets most often, from ASIC’s INFO 266:

SituationWhat’s needed instead
Further advice, after an earlier SOA, where the client’s relevant circumstances and the basis of the advice are not significantly differentA record of advice (ROA), plus the conflicts information an SOA would contain. Keep it for seven years; the client can ask for a copy.
Advice on financial investments worth $15,000 or less in total, with conditions (it doesn’t generally cover derivatives, general insurance or life insurance)A record of advice, which must be given to the client.
Advice that doesn’t recommend acquiring or disposing of a product, where you and certain associates receive no remuneration or benefit for itA record of advice, given to the client on request.

What must a statement of advice include?

RG 175.96 lists what every SOA must contain:

  • the title ‘Statement of Advice’ on the cover, or at or near the front
  • the providing entity’s name and contact details, and its AFS licence number if it’s a licensee
  • for an authorised representative: the authorising licensee’s details, and a statement that you act as its authorised representative
  • a statement setting out the advice
  • the basis on which the advice is given
  • remuneration, commission and other benefits that might reasonably be expected to influence the advice, including benefits for referrals
  • any interests, associations or relationships that might reasonably be expected to influence the advice
  • if the advice is based on incomplete or inaccurate information, the warning that s961H requires

The level of detail is what a person would reasonably need, as a retail client, to decide whether to follow the advice (s947B(3)). ASIC adds that the SOA should be dated and normally say how long the recommendation stays current.

For the basis of the advice, ASIC expects the reasoning in language that’s easy to understand (RG 175.105): the subject matter and scope, a concise summary of the client’s relevant circumstances, a general description of the products or strategies considered, why the advice is appropriate given the alternatives, its advantages and disadvantages, and how you acted in the client’s best interests. Where tax was considered, say so, and describe the significant risks (RG 175.107).

If you recommend replacing one product with another, s947D adds more: the cost of the switch, benefits the client may lose, and any other significant consequences.

A summary, not the law

This summarises ASIC’s guidance as it stood when we checked it. Read Section C of RG 175 in full, and confirm your template with your licensee.

How long should a statement of advice be?

The law sets no page count. It says the SOA must be worded and presented in a clear, concise and effective manner (s947B(6)), and ASIC says presentation is as important as content. In RG 175.126 to RG 175.129, ASIC expects you to:

  • keep all key information in the body, not in an appendix
  • tailor it to the client, leaving out generic research and educational material
  • give a short and simple SOA for short and simple advice
  • highlight the most important information in an executive summary, especially when the SOA runs past 10 pages
  • avoid jargon, and explain in plain English any technical term you can’t avoid

Concise doesn’t mean leaving out required information. It means presenting it as briefly as you reasonably can without compromising accuracy.

Long SOAs are a known problem. Treasury’s Quality of Advice Review (2022) reported that advisers universally criticised SOAs for being too complex and adding significantly to the cost of advice, and that consumers find them too long.

What should an SOA template include?

ASIC’s RG 90 contains an example SOA for limited personal insurance advice. ASIC says it’s a model to adapt, not a template to copy. This structure follows it; adapt it to your licensee’s requirements:

  1. Cover: ‘Statement of Advice’, the clients’ names, the date, the adviser, the licensee and licence number, and what the document is for.
  2. Contents: especially once it runs past 10 pages.
  3. Summary: the recommendations, and the fees, costs and commissions, with page references.
  4. What you want: the client’s goals, in their own words where you can.
  5. What this advice covers: the scope, what’s excluded and why, and what that means for them.
  6. About you: a concise summary of their relevant circumstances.
  7. My advice: each recommendation, stated clearly and unambiguously.
  8. Why: reasons tied to their goals, the options considered, and the advantages and disadvantages.
  9. Consequences and risks: including switching costs and lost benefits where relevant, and any incomplete-information warning.
  10. Fees, costs and benefits: what you and others receive, and any conflicts.
  11. How to go ahead: the steps, and an authority to proceed.

RG 90’s example leaves out blanket disclaimers, product information and anything not material to the advice. The detail of options you considered and rejected sits on the client file; the SOA gives a general description and your reasons.

SOA or record of advice? A worked example

Worked example (fictional)

Last year Mara Holloway, an adviser at Aynub Financial in Kingaroy, gave Priya and Arjun Shah an SOA on retirement and super, built around retiring at 60 and 63. At this year’s review, Priya asks whether she should increase her salary sacrifice. On its own, that might have been further advice that a record of advice could cover.

But Arjun mentions his employer is restructuring and he may move to part-time work. His income, and possibly his retirement date, are now uncertain, so the Shahs’ relevant circumstances may be significantly different from those in last year’s SOA. Mara doesn’t rely on a record of advice. She updates the fact find, waits for Arjun’s latest super statement, models both scenarios, and gives them a new, short SOA on the contribution question.

Three months later, nothing has changed and the Shahs ask Mara to confirm the contribution amount still stands. After confirming their circumstances are the same, she records that further advice in a record of advice that refers to the new SOA by date, as RG 175.95 allows.

What does a statement of advice cost to produce?

There’s no official figure for what an SOA costs to produce. The Quality of Advice Review did find that preparing an SOA is time consuming and adds to the cost of advice. Time usually goes on:

  • Chasing information: statements, payslips and answers that should have been in the fact find.
  • Modelling: rebuilding scenarios that started in someone else’s spreadsheet.
  • Research: comparing options and recording why some were rejected.
  • Writing: client-specific sections, plus standard wording that still needs checking.
  • Checking: paraplanning, adviser review and any licensee pre-vetting, and each round of corrections.

To find your own cost, follow three recent SOAs from strategy meeting to signed authority, recording hours by person and stage and each round of corrections, then price those hours. That number, not an industry average, shows you where to act first.

AI can now draft an SOA in your template from a confirmed fact find, the modelling and the adviser’s strategy. It takes away much of the writing, not the judgement: the adviser still decides, checks and approves the advice, and stays responsible for it. Common questions

Does general advice need a statement of advice?

No. The SOA requirement applies to personal advice given to retail clients. General advice has its own obligations, including giving the client a general advice warning.

How long must you keep a statement of advice?

At least seven years after the day the advice is given, under notional section 912G. ASIC says any document referred to in the SOA must be kept with it for the same period.

What happens if an SOA is defective?

Giving a defective SOA, one that is misleading or deceptive or leaves out required material, is an offence, and so is failing to give an SOA when one is required. An affected client may also take civil action for loss or damage.

Can an SOA refer to another document instead of repeating it?

Often, yes. The SOA must identify the document, it must already have been given to the client or be given with the SOA, and the client must be able to get a free copy on request. This doesn’t apply to switching information under s947D or the s961H warning.

Does a client have to sign the statement of advice?

ASIC’s RG 90 describes an authority to proceed as general industry practice, and its example SOA includes one with a checklist for the client. Check what your licensee requires.
