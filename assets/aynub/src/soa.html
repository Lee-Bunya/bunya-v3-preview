Our opening statement

Where you are, and where this goes.

Sam and Jo, you have sought our guidance to establish a number of key outcomes: how do two people on a strong combined income, carrying $2,910,000 of property debt, stop depending on that income altogether?

You set the target yourself, around $100,000 a year of passive income in today’s dollars, and you set the deadline: age 60 at the outside, earlier if we can show it works. Everything we have modelled is our thought process to get you there without asking you to live smaller in the meantime.

Our conclusion

The pathway that serves you best turns a leveraged property into capital, and capital into ownership, without a new loan at any point along the way. Repair and sell 21 Range View Road, clear its Tidewater loan, keep a $200,000 cash reserve, and buy Sam’s parents’ Ridgeview Farm progressively, one-third in 2028 and the rest from surplus cash flow rather than from the bank.

The product advice that sits alongside it

Move both Kestrel accounts into Tessera Super, wind up the dormant SMSF, and build part of the wealth you will live on before age 60 in an investment bond as well as in super. The bond can be drawn on before age 60, which super cannot.

The rest of this guide sets out how we got there, short version first: what the advice sets out to solve, what we considered, what we discounted, and where we landed.

The Range View decision

One choice, two credible pathways.

Everything in this advice hangs off one choice. You want to own Sam’s parents’ property, Ridgeview Farm, and the way you get there runs through 21 Range View Road, the house you are renting out now. Your $330,000 of offset savings covers the first costs of either pathway. This is the conversation to have before you read anything else.

Our recommendationPathway A

Improve it, then sell it

  • Commit up to $200,000 to the structural work you flagged, the retaining walls and the driveway, and bring the property up to a saleable standard.
  • Take it to market in 2027. At our assumed $3,550,000 sale price, the proceeds repay the $2,190,000 Tidewater loan secured by it, removing the largest drag on your cash flow.
  • What is left buys the first one-third of Ridgeview Farm in 2028. The rest follows in instalments, modelled to finish in 2039–40.
  • Critically, those later instalments are funded from future cash flow, not from taking on more debt. You are converting a leveraged asset into capital and then into ownership, step by step.
The alternativePathway B

Knock down, rebuild, then move in

  • A knock-down rebuild of around $1,000,000, funded by new borrowings on top of the $2,190,000 Tidewater loan.
  • You keep the land and end up in a brand new home in the suburb you want to be in. That is a real and legitimate outcome.
  • But to service that loan, you would need to live with Sam’s parents at Ridgeview Farm for the entire construction period. Not part of it: all of it. The cash flow does not stretch to paying rent and servicing the rebuild at the same time.
  • It also means more debt in the very years you want debt coming down. The model needs extra borrowing in the early years.

Why an investment bond

Earnings are taxed inside the bond at up to 30%. After ten years, withdrawals are generally tax-free and stay out of your tax return.

Withdraw sooner and the earnings are taxed at your marginal rate, less a 30% offset: all of them in years one to eight, two-thirds in year nine, one-third in year ten. Paying in more than 125% of the previous year’s amount, or skipping a year then paying in again, restarts the ten years.

What we are recommending, continued

We recommend keeping $200,000 on call.

First for the Range View repairs, then as your standing reserve.

Cash reserve · joint names
$200,000

You told us in early August that 21 Range View Road needs significant capital repairs, retaining walls and the driveway, costing anywhere from $25,000 to $200,000, and that you want a reasonable amount kept available. Today $200,000 of your offset savings covers the top of that range; after the sale a fresh $200,000 is set aside (page 11).

  • We deliberately did not recommend using that money to cut loan principal. Once it is paid into the loan it is not easily yours again; in the offset it is.
  • While it sits there it is still working. Every dollar in the offset reduces the interest on the loan it sits against, with no market risk. The loans are on rental properties, so the interest saved also trims your deductions.
  • It is held at call, not in the market. Subject to the account terms and the Financial Claims Scheme limit, you are less likely to sell growth assets at a bad moment to pay a builder.
  • The same dollars are never counted twice. Repairs come from today’s savings; the 2028 reserve comes from the sale and sits against the Southgate loan on 18 Marlow Street.
  • One trade-off to be clear about: its only return is the interest saved, so over long periods the reserve may not keep pace with inflation. That is the price of keeping it available, and it is why we have capped it at $200,000 rather than leaving your whole offset balance in cash.
Why this is in your interest

This is the balance you asked for. At-call cash stays liquid and earmarked for the repairs, and everything above it goes to work in tax-effective long-term investments rather than sitting idle.
