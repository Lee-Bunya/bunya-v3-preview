01
Why advice

What your advice fee pays for.

A fee is easy to put a number on. The value of advice is harder to see, so we’ve set it out plainly.

Some of it can be measured: a portfolio that suits you, tax arranged in the right order, contributions made at the right time. Much of it can’t. It’s the phone call when markets fall, the question you didn’t know to ask, and the plan that’s still standing when life changes direction.

None of that appears as a line on your statement. You won’t see the decision you didn’t make in a panic, or the opportunity you were ready for because the groundwork was laid years earlier.

You see it in your life instead: in the choices you have, the time you get back, and the quiet confidence of knowing someone is keeping the whole picture in view.

Money is only ever a means. Good advice helps you put it to work for the life you actually want.

04
Staying the course

The hardest part is staying put.

Coaching you through the rough patches is one of the most valuable things an adviser does, because investing is as much about people as it is about markets.

When prices fall and every headline sounds worse than the last, worry is a reasonable response. Selling up and waiting for calmer days can feel like the careful thing to do. The trouble is that some of the market’s best days tend to arrive right when confidence is lowest, so a decision made out of fear can cost far more than the fall itself.

Feeling nervous about your money doesn’t make you a bad investor. It makes you normal.

Our job is to put some distance between how you feel this week and what you decide for the next ten years. We take you back to why the plan was built the way it was, and to the goals that haven’t changed just because the market has.

Often the most valuable move is no move at all.

The advice you never see

That conversation won’t show up on any statement. In a hard week, it can be the most valuable advice we give.

05
Beyond the numbers

The parts of a plan that never show a return, but often matter most.

Personal insurance

Life, TPD, trauma and income protection cover earns nothing in the years you don’t need it. In the year you do, it’s the difference between keeping the house or the farm and selling it, between the kids staying at their school and moving, and between a plan that survives and one that comes undone in a few months.

Tax structuring

Whose name an asset is in, the order of contributions and withdrawals, when a gain is realised, and how super, investment bonds, trusts and companies are used all decide how much of what you build reaches you, and the people you want it to reach.

Why it matters

None of this feels urgent, which is exactly why it gets put off, and why mistakes here usually surface years later, when they’re hardest to fix.

One plan, not a pile of separate ones

Retirement, insurance, debt, school fees, ageing parents, aged care and estate planning aren’t separate jobs. Change one and you change what’s possible in the others. We look at all of it together, put decisions in the right order, and keep the plan aimed at the life you want rather than at the market.

Good advice is rarely one big call. It’s many sensible decisions working together, season after season.

The whole picture

What it all adds up to.

Markets will rise and fall, and nobody can tell you when. What you can have is a clear plan, and people you trust to help you through whatever comes.

For us, advice has never been only about investments. It starts with the life you want: where you want to live, who you want to look after, and what you hope to make possible.

From there our job is to make each decision clearer, keep it connected to the bigger picture, and help you stay focused on what matters to you.

What counts isn’t the size of the balance. It’s what it lets you do with your life.

We don’t try to predict what markets will do next. We bring the discipline, perspective and careful planning that keep you moving toward your goals through changing markets, changing circumstances and every stage of life.

It’s knowing someone has the whole picture in view, is thinking ahead, and adjusts your plan as your life changes, so every decision still points the same way.

Plan backwards. Live forwards.

That’s what we mean by financial certainty: living well now, looking ahead with confidence, and leaving something that lasts for the people who come after you.

Example03Graham and Lorraine’s gift

Graham and Lorraine sold their peanut farm near Kingaroy and retired last year. To help their daughter Jess buy her first home, they gave her $60,000 toward a deposit. What they didn’t know is that Centrelink treats gifts above the limits as a ‘deprived asset’ for five years, so most of that $60,000 still counts against their Age Pension even though it’s gone. Spreading the gifts within the limits over the five years before they applied would have avoided it.

Example05Ray’s recontribution strategy

Ray is 63 and has just retired from the council. Most of his super is taxable component. Because he’s met a condition of release, he can withdraw some of it tax-free and put it back in as a non-concessional contribution, within the contribution caps. The money that goes back in counts as tax-free component. Ray’s own pension is already tax-free because he’s over 60, so the real benefit is for his adult children: if they inherit his super, they pay no tax on the tax-free component, compared with up to 17% on the taxable component.

Example07Luke and Amy’s insurance review

Luke and Amy took out life, TPD and trauma cover twelve years ago, when they’d borrowed heavily to buy a block outside Kingaroy and build. The cover was set to clear the loan if either of them died or couldn’t work. Since then the loan has shrunk, their super has grown and the kids are older. At their review we reduced the cover to what they actually need now. Their premiums fell sharply, leaving more in their super and more in the family budget each month.

Premiums matter more than most people think. In 2018 the Productivity Commission estimated that for people on low incomes or with broken work patterns, insurance premiums paid from super could cut their retirement balance by 14%, or about $85,000, and by as much as 28%, or $125,000, for some members.

Example08A head start for Oliver

Bill and Jan’s first grandson, Oliver, was born this year, and they want to help him with university or a first home one day. Both are on the top marginal tax rate. Investing in their own names would mean tax at 47%, including the Medicare levy, on interest, and investing in Oliver’s name would attract the high tax rates on a child’s unearned income. An investment bond pays tax inside the bond at up to 30%, and after ten years it can be withdrawn with no further tax. Bill and Jan keep control until Oliver is ready, and can add to it each year.
