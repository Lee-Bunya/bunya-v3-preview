The short answer

How should a financial advice firm approve social media posts?

Social media approval for financial advisers works best as a simple route: brief, draft, review, approve, schedule and check. Review the whole post as readers will see it, including the image and link, record approval against that exact version, and send any later change back for review. AI can draft; people stay responsible.

Key takeaways
  • ASIC’s updated RG 234 (June 2026) covers advertising on social media, and applies the same law to AI-written content.
  • Approve the exact version that goes live: a changed graphic or caption is a new post.
  • Keep client names, ages and dollar figures out of drafts, and keep a record of what was published.

Why do financial advisers need a social media approval process?

It’s 4:50 on a Friday. A LinkedIn post about retirement planning is scheduled for Monday morning. The principal said “looks good” in a Teams chat on Tuesday. Since then someone has swapped the graphic, and it now reads “Retire five years earlier”. Nobody has looked at it again.

That’s the gap social media approval for financial advisers is meant to close. Not a heavy compliance machine, just clear answers to three questions: which version are we reviewing, who can approve it, and what is actually scheduled to go live?

The stakes are real. ASIC’s advertising guidance applies to any communication that informs people about, or promotes, financial advice services, whatever the medium. A firm that can show who approved which version, and on what basis, is in a far better position than one relying on a chat thread.

What does ASIC say about advertising financial advice on social media?

ASIC’s main guidance is Regulatory Guide 234, reissued in June 2026. It is guidance to help promoters, including financial advisers, avoid false or misleading statements and misleading or deceptive conduct. The points most relevant to an advice firm’s social media:

  • Every medium is covered. The guide applies to advertising in any medium, including social media, whatever technology is used to make it, including AI (RG 234.5).
  • AI content meets the same standard. ASIC notes that the law applies to AI-generated advertising as it does to human-written content, and that hallucinated or biased output may increase the risk of misleading advertising.
  • Claims need support. Claims about client outcomes should be substantiated, and promoters should keep records that support them (RG 234.19).
  • The overall impression counts. For posts, reels and short videos, consider the impression the content creates when first seen. If a format has no room for balanced information, reconsider the format (RG 234.176).
  • A link doesn’t fix a headline. Don’t rely on a click-through link to carry warnings or qualifications, particularly on platforms such as Facebook and Instagram (RG 234.173 and 234.174).
  • Don’t oversell the service. Advertising for advice shouldn’t create unrealistic expectations, misrepresent an adviser’s experience, or overstate what digital or AI tools in the service can do (RG 234.151 to 234.153).
  • Keep a record. Promoters should keep a record of their advertising, including any disclaimers or warnings (RG 234.177).

If your firm works with influencers, ASIC’s INFO 269 says licensees may be liable for misconduct by influencers they use, and expects due diligence and monitoring. It also reminds anyone posting that a statement of opinion intended, or reasonably seen as intended, to influence a decision about a financial product can be financial product advice.

A workflow isn’t a compliance sign-off

An approval process organises review and responsibility. It doesn’t make a post compliant on its own. Your licensee’s policy decides which content needs which level of review.

What does a simple approval workflow look like?

Five steps, each with an owner and a record:

  1. Brief: the audience, topic, purpose, channel, date and source material.
  2. Draft: the wording, visual and link for each channel.
  3. Review: the complete post checked against the firm’s content rules and the checklist below.
  4. Approve: an authorised person approves an identifiable version, with any conditions.
  5. Schedule and check: the approved version goes out, someone confirms the live post, and replies are monitored.

In a small firm one person may hold several roles, but write them down anyway:

RoleResponsible forLeaves a record of
Content ownerThe brief and the complete review package.Draft version and sources.
ReviewerAccuracy, presentation and anything needing escalation.Feedback and open questions.
ApproverThe publication decision, within the firm’s policy.Approved version, name, date and conditions.
PublisherScheduling the approved version and checking it live.Channel, time and live link.
Response ownerReplies, enquiries and escalations.Follow-up actions taken.

Set a backup approver and a route for urgent posts, so a deadline never becomes an informal approval rule.

What should you check before a post goes live?

Review the package as your audience will see it: image, headline, caption, link preview and landing page. A careful caption doesn’t fix an unsupported claim in the graphic.

  • Purpose: is it clear who the post is for and what they should do next?
  • Claims: can every fact, number, comparison and outcome be supported by a current source you’ve kept?
  • Balance: do qualifications and risks sit with the claim they relate to, in the post itself rather than behind a link?
  • Advice boundaries: could it be read as a recommendation to a particular person? Does it need extra review under your licensee’s policy?
  • Identity: are the firm name, adviser details and any required disclosures correct?
  • Privacy: is any client story, photo or testimonial authorised for this exact use, with identifying details removed?
  • Images: does the visual imply something the words don’t, such as a lifestyle outcome?
  • Version and timing: is this the approved version, and will it still be accurate on the scheduled date?
  • Follow-up: who will watch the replies, and who can pause or correct the post?

Can an advice firm use AI to write social media posts?

Yes, with care. AI is good at turning an adviser’s idea, a voice memo or an article into a version for each channel. Give it approved source material, the audience, the firm’s voice and the limits of the brief, and ask it to flag missing evidence rather than invent figures or examples.

Then review the result as closely as anything a person wrote, because the law treats it the same way. Check whether the draft has exaggerated a benefit, dropped a qualification or implied an outcome the source didn’t support. Repeat the check for each shorter or channel-specific version; trimming a caption can change its meaning.

The biggest practical risk is privacy. A post inspired by a real client conversation can carry identifying detail into a draft without anyone noticing: a first name, an age, a balance. Keep confidential client material out of tools your firm hasn’t approved, remove names, ages and dollar figures before review, and label any example that’s fictional. The same principle, AI drafts and a person approves, applies elsewhere in the practice too, as in AI meeting notes.

What happens when a post changes after approval?

Worked example (fictional)

After a review meeting, Mara Holloway of Aynub Financial, a fee-for-service practice in Kingaroy, Queensland, records a voice memo: couples who plan to retire at different ages often haven’t thought about the years in between. It would make a good post.

The first draft opens with “A couple we met this week, aged 58 and 61, with $900,000 in super…”. The reviewer removes the ages and the figure: even without names, those details could identify the clients to people who know them. The revised blog, LinkedIn and Facebook versions are approved one by one, and the newsletter version is held back for December.

On Friday, a colleague swaps the LinkedIn graphic for one reading “Retire five years earlier”. That’s a new outcome claim, so the publisher returns it for review instead of relying on Tuesday’s approval. The line comes out, the new version is approved and recorded, and on Monday the response owner checks the live post and its link.

The workflow kept a clear line between each decision and the content that actually appeared.

What records should you keep of social media approvals?

A comment saying “looks good” is hard to interpret six months later if the content has changed since. For each post, keep together:

  • the approved text and creative for each channel;
  • who approved it, when, and any conditions;
  • the sources behind any claims or figures;
  • where and when it was published, with a live link;
  • any correction or withdrawal, the reason and who authorised it.

Define what counts as a change that needs fresh review. At a minimum, changed claims, figures, audience, images, links or context should go back to the approver. Agree retention and access with whoever is responsible for the firm’s obligations; a content calendar alone rarely keeps this history. Who handles comments, corrections and paused posts?

The work continues after scheduling. Someone should confirm the right version went live, the link works and replies are being handled. Never discuss a person’s financial circumstances in a public comment thread; move the conversation to a private channel through the firm’s normal process.

Keep a way to pause scheduled content when markets move, rules change or a source goes out of date, and agree who can authorise a correction. Track waiting time for review, revision rounds and missed dates; they reveal process problems faster than likes do.

Your next step: take the last five posts your firm published and check whether you could show, for each one, who approved the version that went live. If you can’t, use the five-step route above for your next post.

Common questions

Does every social media post need the same level of approval?

No. Your firm should set the review route by content type and risk, consistent with your licensee’s requirements. A routine event reminder and a post about retirement strategies rarely carry the same risk.

Is an educational post still advertising?

It can be. ASIC’s guidance applies to communications that inform people about or promote financial advice services, so don’t assume an educational label removes the need for review. Your licensee’s policy sets the level.

Can AI approve financial advice marketing?

No. AI can help check a draft against rules your firm defines, but approval is a judgement that needs an accountable person. Keep an escalation route for anything that needs advice or compliance input.

Should each channel version be approved separately?

Review the actual material for each channel, because shortening a caption or changing a graphic can alter the message. You can group versions in one review package, as long as each is clearly identified and approved.

Can advisers share firm content on their own social accounts?

Check your licensee’s policy first. If an adviser’s post promotes the firm or its services, treat it like any other firm content: approved before it goes out, and kept on record.

Does an approval workflow make our content compliant?

No. It organises review and responsibility. The content itself still needs to meet the applicable requirements, assessed by the appropriate people.
