Advice File Review Checklist: Records a Reviewer Can Follow

When ASIC or your licensee asks for a client file, they read it as a whole. Make sure it tells the story.

The short answer

How do you prepare an advice file for review?

Use an advice file review checklist and test the file the way a reviewer would. Can someone who didn’t do the work find the client’s circumstances, the information relied on, the advice, who approved it, fee consents and open actions? ASIC considers the client file as a whole, so fix gaps where records are created, not the week before.

Key takeaways
  • ASIC asks licensees for the client file and considers it as a whole, not one document in isolation.
  • Keep records of advice for at least seven years, and keep records of the information you relied on.
  • Test a sample with someone who didn’t do the work, then fix repeat gaps where the record is created.

What happens when a reviewer asks for a client file?

The email lands on a Tuesday. Your licensee’s file review has picked five clients, and the files are due Friday. The Statements of Advice are easy to find. But one couple’s fact find is in a former paraplanner’s OneDrive, the signed authority to proceed is an email attachment nobody saved, and the note from the strategy meeting is still in the adviser’s notebook.

That week is avoidable. ASIC’s records of advice FAQs say that when it reviews advice for compliance, it asks the licensee to produce the client’s file, doesn’t look at a record of advice in isolation, and considers the full file as a whole. It makes sense to prepare for your licensee’s reviews the same way.

An advice file review checklist helps, but only if you use it before the request arrives. The aim is a file another person can follow without you in the room.

What does a reviewer need to follow?

A useful file explains the sequence: what was intended, what happened, who acted and what’s still unresolved. Collecting more documents doesn’t make that clearer on its own. Connecting them does.

ASIC’s guidance says you must keep records of the information you relied on and, where you rely on earlier advice, the file should show the inquiries you made. Those records typically include more than the advice documents themselves. ASIC also says records of advice should be kept for at least seven years after the advice is given, and that records don’t have to be paper based: a record of advice can be kept in writing or as an audio or video recording.

Agree with the people responsible for governance which records your firm keeps, where they live and who reviews them. Your licensee may set requirements beyond ASIC’s.

Where should each record live?

Give every type of record one agreed home: client documents, meeting recordings and notes, fact finds, advice documents, approvals and fee consents. Use names and dates the team can keep up in daily work, and link each item to the client it belongs to.

Records in personal drives and inboxes are the usual gap. When someone leaves, their files can become hard to reach. Check that links open for the authorised people who need them. If they don’t, fix the location or the permissions. Don’t solve it by sharing a file more widely than it needs to be. An advice file review checklist you can use today

Ask an authorised colleague who didn’t do the work to open a file and answer each question:

CheckQuestion for the reviewer
ContextCan I identify the client, their circumstances and the scope of the advice?
Information relied onCan I see the information the adviser relied on, and the inquiries made?
SequenceDo dates and records explain what happened, in order?
Source accessCan I open every supporting record with my authorised access?
VersionsIs it clear which version of each document was reviewed and given to the client?
DecisionsCan I see who approved the advice, and what happened next?
FeesAre fee disclosures and any consents on file, signed and dated?
ExceptionsAre unresolved items visible, each with an owner and a next action?

Then make it a routine:

  1. Select a sample using your firm’s agreed approach, including files with exceptions, not just the tidy ones.
  2. Open the supporting links and check the referenced versions.
  3. Separate reviewed sources from drafts, summaries and later reconstructions.
  4. Log each gap, without hiding it under a completion label.
  5. Give each finding an owner and a follow-up date.
  6. Recheck the fix, and look for the same issue in other files.

This is an operational checklist, not an audit standard or a complete list of legal requirements. For fee consent specifics, see ongoing fee arrangement renewal.

What does a practice file review look like?

Worked example (fictional)

At Aynub Financial in Kingaroy, adviser Mara Holloway runs a practice review every quarter. This time she picks three files from the last three months, including one where the client changed direction halfway through. The practice manager, who worked on none of them, follows each file using the checklist.

The first file holds up. In the second, a super statement the fact find relied on opens only for the paraplanner who saved it. The fix is to move it to the client’s record, not to share the original more widely. In the third, the authority to proceed is signed but undated. The team doesn’t add a date after the fact. They record the finding, follow the licensee’s process, and add a ‘signed and dated’ check before any advice goes out.

A quarter later, Mara samples three new files. The undated-signature problem hasn’t come back.

The lesson: a file review finds problems, but the fix belongs where the record is created.

Can AI help without manufacturing evidence?

AI can summarise supplied records, point a reviewer to the right document and flag items worth investigating. When advice is drafted with AI, as in our guide to AI Statement of Advice drafting, linking each figure to its source makes the file easier to follow. The reviewer still inspects the underlying sources and checks the summary against them.

If an explanation is written later, label it as a later explanation, never as a note made at the time. Keep uncertainties visible. A plausible story doesn’t show that an event happened or that a decision was approved.

How do you know your files are getting easier to review?

Track a few measures across each sample: the time it takes to find sources, broken or restricted links, missing approval records and findings that repeat. Compare similar files over time against your own baseline. Better organisation makes a review easier, but it doesn’t guarantee any particular finding.

Your next step: before the end of the month, run the checklist on three recent files, including one that didn’t go to plan. Fix the most common gap at the point where that record is created.

Common questions

Is a single document folder enough for a file review?

It helps to have one agreed location, but a reviewer also needs context, the right versions, access and an explanation of open items. The links between records matter as much as the records themselves.

Should we copy everything into a separate audit folder?

Use the method agreed for the review. If you need a snapshot or export, make its scope and date clear, and don’t lose the link to the source records. A second folder won’t stay in step on its own.

How long do records of advice need to be kept?

ASIC’s guidance says records of advice should be kept for at least seven years after the advice is given. Your licensee may set further requirements, so confirm them for every record type you keep.

Can a meeting recording be part of the advice record?

ASIC says a record of advice can be kept as an audio or video recording or in writing, and records don’t have to be paper based. Make sure any recording is linked to the client and kept for the required period.

What should we do if a file review finds a missing record?

Record the gap, give it an owner and follow your licensee’s process. Don’t recreate the record and present it as if it were made at the time; if you write a later explanation, label it as one.

Can a system make us audit-ready automatically?

No. A system can support filing, permissions and approvals. People still define the requirements, check the record and investigate gaps, and no software status guarantees compliance.
