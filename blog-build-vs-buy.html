Build vs buy software for advice firms: how to decide, and a third path

Buying can mean bending to someone else’s roadmap. Building means owning every fix. There’s a middle path worth testing too.

The short answer

Should a financial advice firm build or buy its software?

In build vs buy software decisions, buy when a product handles your essential workflows and you can live with its roadmap. Build when important needs stay unmet and you can fund testing, maintenance and support for years. Many firms should also test a third path: a standard base they shape themselves, compared on the same workflows, cost and exit terms.

Key takeaways
  • Test every option on the same real workflow, including a missing document and a handover.
  • Owning a system doesn’t remove licences, maintenance or the need for someone to run it.
  • Ask who can make a small change, how long it takes and who approves it before it goes live.

Why does build vs buy software come up for advice firms?

Your practice manager asks for one new field on the client form: the client’s preferred contact time. The supplier says it’s on the roadmap. A developer you know says he could build you something better over a few weekends. Both answers should make you a little nervous.

That’s the build vs buy software question in miniature. Buying gives you a tested product and a supplier who maintains it, but you work the way the product works. Building gives you exactly what you ask for, but every fix, update and handover becomes your firm’s job, for as long as you use it.

Neither is automatically better. The right answer depends on how well each option fits your work, what it costs over its life, and who is responsible when something changes.

What jobs must the system do for your firm?

Write down the workflows that matter most, the people involved and the information each person needs. For most advice firms that means the client record, the path from first meeting to implemented advice, reviews and fee consent, and the evidence of what was done.

Separate what’s essential from what’s a preference the team could change. Then test the awkward moments, not just the ideal path in a demo:

  • A client hands over a document late, or not at all.
  • An adviser goes on leave and their work is handed to someone else.
  • A licensee changes a standard and a form needs a new question.

When does each path make sense?

  • Buy when a product already handles your essential workflows, the supplier’s roadmap suits you, and you’re comfortable working its way.
  • Build when important requirements stay unmet by anything on the market, and the firm can fund design, testing, maintenance and support for the life of the system.
  • Shape when most of your work is common to advice firms but your stages, roles, approval points and templates are your own, and you want small changes requested by your own team and approved by you, rather than waiting on a supplier’s roadmap.

The shaped path is the approach behind Bunya Command Centre, so weigh what follows with that in mind. Whatever you lean towards, test it the same way as the others.

How do you compare the full cost of each option?

Use one planning period for all three, such as three years, and keep confirmed quotes separate from estimates. Don’t treat uncertain time savings as cash.

  • Initial work: discovery, configuration or build, migration, testing, training and transition.
  • Recurring costs: licences, hosting, integrations, support, usage and storage.
  • Your team’s time: project ownership, data checks, testing and training.
  • Change and continuity: improvements, maintenance, documentation and handover.
  • Exit: data extraction, replacement integrations and any contract charges.

Owning a system doesn’t make Microsoft licences, third-party services or support free. For a line-by-line worksheet, use the financial advice software cost checklist.

What does owning the system actually mean?

Ownership is a contract question, not a deployment one. Ask what you can configure, who can make changes and how your data can be exported. For a custom build, establish which components, documentation, access and rights are delivered to you, and what another developer would need to know to maintain it.

Ask where client records and documents are stored and who administers access. A system that runs inside your firm’s own environment changes the conversation about data, but it still needs someone accountable for access, integrations and updates. Worked example: one review workflow, three ways

Worked example (fictional)

Aynub Financial, a fictional fee-for-service practice in Kingaroy, Queensland, wants reviews to stop slipping. Its principal adviser, Mara Holloway, writes five acceptance criteria: every review has an owner, it starts from the client’s real dates, the supporting documents are easy to find, the fee consent renewal is handled with the review, and exceptions stay visible.

  1. The product. The demo meets four criteria. Linking the fee consent renewal to the review needs a change from the supplier, with no date.
  2. The build. A developer’s prototype meets all five on paper. Mara asks who maintains it when the developer moves on, and nobody has an answer.
  3. The shaped base. It meets four out of the box. Her practice manager asks for the fifth in plain English, sees it as a draft and Mara approves it.

Mara then compares the three-year cost and exit terms for each. The point isn’t which option wins in this example. It’s that she decided from evidence on her own workflow, not from a feature list. If you want help keeping that cycle on track, see the client review workflow checklist.

What should you check before you commit?

  • Have all options been tested against the same essential workflows?
  • Are exclusions and manual steps written down?
  • Who can make a small change, how long does it take, and who approves it?
  • Are migration quality and acceptance criteria defined?
  • Are licences, usage and support separated from setup costs?
  • Who owns the project inside the firm, and how much time can they give?
  • Who maintains each integration and handles failures after go-live?
  • Does the contract reflect the ownership, handover and exit terms you discussed?

Common questions

Is building software always more expensive than buying?

There’s no universal answer. Compare real proposals over the same period, including your team’s time and ongoing costs. A lower initial quote can leave more work or cost for later.

Does owning the system mean there are no monthly fees?

No. An owned system can still need Microsoft licences, third-party services, usage charges and support. Ask for every ongoing dependency and its cost in writing.

Can AI make a custom build maintenance-free?

No. AI can help with defined development or day-to-day tasks, but the system still needs testing, access management, integration upkeep and accountable people.

Can a firm combine buying and building?

Yes. A tailored workflow can sit on a standard platform and connect to products you keep. The important work is agreeing the boundaries, the costs and who maintains each part.

Who should own the software decision in a small advice firm?

One named person, usually the principal or practice manager, with time set aside for it. They gather the workflows, run the tests and keep the comparison honest.

What should happen to our data if we change systems later?

Your agreement should say what you can export, in what format, at what cost and with what help. Check this before you sign, not when you want to leave.
