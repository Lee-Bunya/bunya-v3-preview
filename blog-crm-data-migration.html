The short answer

What should a CRM data migration checklist for an advice firm include?

A CRM data migration checklist should cover an inventory of every record type and its owner, mapping rules agreed by meaning, a trial run on a sample, checks that relationships, documents, access and open tasks survive, a planned cutover with a fallback, and secure handling of exports. Agree how old records stay retrievable before switching the old system off.

Key takeaways
  • Record counts can match while trusts, documents and open tasks go missing; test real workflows too.
  • Decide how much history to move: start clean, bring active clients, or bring everything.
  • Treat every export file as client data: controlled access, an approved transfer channel and a deletion date.

Why do CRM migrations go wrong when the numbers match?

The import report says 1,200 of 1,200 records. Everyone relaxes. Then on Monday an adviser opens a client and the family trust has disappeared, the last SOA links back to the old system, and the review due next week has no owner.

A CRM data migration checklist exists to catch exactly that. An advice firm’s CRM holds more than contacts: household and entity relationships, file notes, documents, service records, fee arrangements, open tasks and links to other systems. The first job is deciding what the new system must keep, and what can stay readable in an archive.

Give each record type a business owner. Ask them to show how the team uses it today, including the exceptions. A field that looks unused may feed a report, and an old document link may be the only route to a historical advice record.

What goes in a migration inventory?

For each item, record the source, owner, export method, destination, transformation rules and the check that proves it worked. Include anything that will stay in an archive, with a named way to get it back.

How much client history should you move?

Not every firm needs to move everything. There are three common choices:

  • Start clean: new work begins in the new system and the old one becomes a read-only archive. Quickest, but the team works in two places when they need history.
  • Bring active clients across: current households, open work and recent documents move; older records stay archived. A common middle ground.
  • Bring the full history: everything in one place, at the cost of more mapping, cleanup and checking.

Whichever you choose, an inactive client isn’t automatically a record you can delete. Confirm your retention and retrieval requirements with whoever is responsible for your firm’s obligations before deciding what stays behind.

How do you agree mapping and cleanup rules?

Confirm export rights, formats, supplier help and any charges before setting a date. Get an authorised sample early enough to expose missing fields or export limits.

Map by meaning, not by matching field names. “Active” could mean an active client, an active service agreement or simply a record that isn’t archived. Write down the source meaning and the destination rule.

  • Keep a register linking each old identifier to its new record, so everything can be traced.
  • Decide how blanks, unknown values, date formats and custom categories are handled.
  • Flag suspected duplicates for a person to review; don’t merge people just because they share a name or email address.
  • Separate a deliberate cleanup decision from an import error, with an owner for each exception.

AI can help suggest mappings or spot likely duplicates. Test its suggestions, and have a person approve the rules before they touch client records.

Worked example: the contacts arrived, the work didn’t

Worked example (fictional)

Aynub Financial, a fictional fee-for-service practice in Kingaroy, Queensland, runs a trial import of 240 households. The count matches the export. Then principal adviser Mara Holloway opens a retired couple’s record: their SMSF isn’t linked, their last advice document points to the old system, and their review task has no owner.

Mara adds three checks to the trial: open every relationship link on a sample of households, open documents using an ordinary staff login, and reconcile outstanding tasks by owner and due date. The team fixes the mapping and runs the trial again before the real move. Matching totals tell you nothing is missing. They can’t tell you the records are usable.

How do you check the migrated data is right?

Agree what “done” means before the trial run. Combine automated reconciliation with hands-on checks, including awkward cases such as linked entities, inactive clients and records with a long history.

  1. Reconcile the numbers by record type and status, accounting for deliberate exclusions, merges and rejected records.
  2. Check the meaning of important values, dates and relationship links.
  3. Open the evidence from the client record, not just the import report.
  4. Test access: what each role can see, and what it shouldn’t.
  5. Run a real scenario: find a client, prepare a review, update a task and retrieve an old advice record.
  6. Resolve exceptions: each issue gets an owner and a decision to fix, defer or exclude, and you agree which ones block go-live.

For the review scenario, use the client review workflow checklist. For historical evidence, see the advice file review checklist.

How do you plan the cutover and a fallback?

The final export is a point in time. Decide how changes made after it will be captured, and which system staff use in the meantime. Otherwise a task finished in the old system can reappear as outstanding in the new one.

  • Before: agree the trial outcome, the final export window, a change freeze, training and a support contact.
  • At cutover: reconcile the final transfer, apply permissions and run the critical workflow checks.
  • Go or pause: name the person who approves launch, and the issues that would delay it.
  • If something fails: define the fallback, including how work entered in either system is reconciled.
  • After: track missing records, access problems and unassigned work in one visible issue log.

Don’t end access to the old system until the archive, retrieval route and contract terms have been checked. The software rollout guide covers training and adoption around the same date.

How do you protect client data during a migration?

The OAIC’s APP 11 guidance explains the security obligations of entities covered by the Australian Privacy Principles, including reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss and unauthorised access, modification or disclosure. It also covers destroying or de-identifying information that is no longer needed, subject to exceptions such as legal requirements to keep it.

For the migration, agree who can access exports, where working copies are kept, how they are transferred and when temporary copies are deleted. Use an approved transfer channel and controlled access, not spreadsheets passed around by email.

Your next step: list your systems, the record types in each, the data problems you already know about, and the three workflows your firm must keep running on day one. Use fictional or approved sample information to start. That list is the backbone of any migration quote.

Common questions

Can everything move from our existing CRM?

That depends on your export access, the formats available and the new system’s structure. Ask for a written list of what will move, what will stay in an archive and what can’t be transferred.

How long does a CRM data migration take?

It depends on data quality, document volume, custom fields, supplier help and testing. Ask for a scoped timetable that includes a trial run and an acceptance stage, rather than a generic estimate.

What should a migration quote include?

Discovery, export help, mapping, cleanup, document handling, trial runs, checks, cutover support and post-launch fixes. Check what’s excluded, and what it costs to keep the old system available.

Should we clean the data before migrating?

Fix known problems where practical, using agreed rules. Keep a trail of what changed, and don’t delete or merge records without checking why they exist and how long they must be kept.

Do we need to keep the old CRM after migrating?

Often for a while, or at least an archive of it. Keep access until the archive and a way to retrieve old records have been tested and the contract terms allow you to close it.

Who in the firm should own the migration?

One named person, usually the practice manager, with time set aside to answer questions and check the trial run. Each record type should also have an owner who knows how it’s used.
