Software rollout for financial advice firms: a checklist your team will actually use

The short answer

How do you roll out new software in a financial advice firm?

A good software rollout for financial advice firms starts with the team’s workflows, not the screens. Agree which system holds which work and from when, pilot it with the people who do and receive the work, train each role on realistic tasks, and measure whether work is completed correctly. Keep support close for the first weeks.

Key takeaways
  • Set one working rule before go-live: which system holds which work, from which date.
  • Pilot with the people who do the work and the people who receive it, including your least confident user.
  • Measure completed work, not logins: can each role finish its critical tasks without help?

Why do software rollouts stall in advice firms?

Three weeks after go-live, the practice manager notices something. Someone in client services is keeping a spreadsheet of review dates “just in case”. An adviser is still emailing file notes to herself. The new system works. The rollout didn’t.

A software rollout for financial advice firms changes everyday habits: where someone finds a client record, how work gets assigned, and who you ask when something looks wrong. Training usually covers the screens. It rarely covers those decisions, so under pressure people fall back on what they know.

The usual causes are predictable: two systems left running without a clear rule, training that shows features rather than tasks, nobody owning the questions after launch, and migrated data that doesn’t match what people expect to see. Each one can be fixed before go-live.

What should you decide before go-live?

Start with a short list of the workflows that matter most, such as preparing a client review, assigning a follow-up and finding a document. For each one, write down the owner, the expected result and the handovers between roles.

Then make the working rule explicit. If review tasks live in the new system from 1 March, say so, and say what happens to tasks still open in the old one. Leaving both systems in use without clear boundaries creates the worst outcome: nobody is sure which record to trust.

Decide early how much history moves across. Starting clean, bringing only active clients, or bringing the full history each carry different costs and risks. If you are moving records, use the CRM data migration checklist alongside this plan. And if your firm is with a licensee, find out early whether they need to approve anything before launch.

How do you run a pilot that proves something?

Choose a pilot group that includes people who do the work and people who receive it, with a range of confidence. A smooth demonstration by the project lead is not the same as a smooth Tuesday for the newest member of the team.

  1. Define it. Agree the users, workflows, data and duration.
  2. Set acceptance criteria. What must people be able to complete, and which problems would block launch?
  3. Use realistic scenarios. Include the awkward cases: a couple with separate super funds, a client who changes their mind, a missing document.
  4. Capture friction. Log unclear steps, missing permissions, errors and duplicate work as they happen.
  5. Fix and retest. Run changed workflows again with the people affected before expanding.

A pilot should answer one question: does the process work for the team? Not simply: does the feature exist?

How should you train an advice team on new software?

Train by role and by task. Give each person a short set of scenarios they’ll actually face, let them complete them, then check the result together.

AdviserPrepare a review and check a draft file note.The source, corrections and next action are clear.
Client servicesAssign a task and request missing information.The owner, due date and client link are right.
Practice managerReview outstanding work and resolve a stuck item.The team knows how to escalate.
System ownerHandle an access request and report a fault.The approval route and support contacts are understood.

Video tutorials work best in a simple loop: watch the video for the task, apply it in a practice scenario, check the result, and revisit the video the next time you do it for real. Watching is a starting point. Completing the task shows where someone needs more help.

Train the checking, not just the clicking

Where AI prepares drafts, include a deliberately flawed example in training. Ask the reviewer to find the error, correct it and explain what should happen before the draft is filed or sent. Checking is a skill, and it’s the one that protects your clients.

What does a rollout problem look like in practice?

Worked example (fictional)

Aynub Financial, a fee-for-service practice in Kingaroy, Queensland, is two weeks into piloting its new practice system. After a review meeting, adviser Mara Holloway adds a follow-up task for client services. The team can see the task but can’t tell whether to start it, because Mara’s file note is still a draft waiting for her check.

Nobody did anything wrong. The system allowed the task to exist before the note was approved, and nobody had agreed what that meant.

The team defines two states: draft follow-up and ready to action. Only Mara moves a task from one to the other, once she has approved the note. The training scenario is updated to cover both roles, a one-page guide explains the handover, and the next pilot run tests it from each side. The fix came from clarifying responsibility as much as from configuring software.

What belongs on a go-live checklist?

Copy this into your launch plan and put a name beside each line:

  • Access: everyone has the permissions they need, and restricted access has been tested too.
  • Records: data, documents and open work have passed the agreed checks after migration.
  • Workflows: critical scenarios and known exceptions have been tested end to end.
  • Training: each role has practised its tasks and knows where to find the tutorials.
  • Approvals: any licensee sign-off you need is in writing.
  • Ownership: someone owns launch decisions, support and unresolved issues.
  • The working rule: everyone knows which system to use for what, from which date.
  • Continuity: there’s a plan if something goes wrong on day one.
  • Follow-up: time is booked in the first fortnight to review issues with the team.

Record any known limitations you’re accepting at launch, and who agreed to them.

How do you know new software is actually being used?

Login counts show access, not adoption. After a month, check these against your pre-launch baseline instead:

  • Can each role complete its critical scenarios without help?
  • Are tasks landing with the right owner and a usable next step?
  • How often does work come back for missing information or correction?
  • Is anyone still entering the same information in two places?
  • Which questions keep coming up?

Keep a simple issue log with the workflow, impact, owner and status, and turn repeated questions into short guidance. Protect time for whoever is supporting the rollout; their normal work doesn’t disappear because they’ve become everyone’s first call. Count that time, too, when you work out the total cost of advice software.

If someone keeps using the old process, find out why before treating it as a performance problem. Missing access, an unclear rule or a genuine gap in the system are more common causes than reluctance.

Your next step: write down three workflows, an owner for each, and the date the new system becomes the place that work lives. Share that one page with the team before anyone opens a training video.

Common questions

Should we roll out everything at once?

That depends on how your workflows connect. A staged rollout keeps learning manageable, but the team needs clear rules for any work that crosses the old and new systems while both are running.

Who should be in the pilot group?

Include the roles that do the work and the roles that receive it, with a mix of experience and software confidence. Test the whole handover rather than one person’s screen.

How much training does an advice team need?

Base it on the tasks that are changing, not a fixed number of hours. Use practical completion checks to see who needs more help, rather than treating a watched video as proof that someone can do the task.

What if staff keep using the old system?

Find out why. Missing access, unclear rules, incomplete features or data problems may make the old process feel necessary. Fix those barriers, then restate the agreed transition date.

Do we need our licensee’s approval to change practice software?

Ask early, because requirements differ between licensees. Expect questions about security, where client data is stored, how AI is controlled and how records are kept, particularly if the system prepares advice documents.

Who should own the rollout inside the firm?

One named person with the authority to make process decisions, often the practice manager, plus a backup. They don’t need to be technical, but they do need protected time and a direct line to the vendor’s support.
