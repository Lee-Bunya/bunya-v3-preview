The short answer

What should an advice firm look for in financial planning software?

Look for financial planning software that works as one system: the client record, the advice work, reviews and approvals together, shaped around how your firm works. Most firms piece together a CRM, spreadsheets and separate tools, so the team keeps rebuilding context. Change in steps: start with the workflow that is most backed up, then connect more.

Key takeaways
  • The real cost of a pieced-together stack is the time spent rebuilding context between tools.
  • One system should be shaped around your firm’s stages and approvals, not the other way round.
  • Start with the workflow that holds your advice up most, then keep, connect or replace the rest.

What does a typical financial adviser tech stack look like?

Ten past four on a Thursday, a client rings to ask where their advice is up to. The answer lives in four places: a CRM note from the meeting, a spreadsheet tracking SOAs, an email thread with the paraplanner, and a document in a shared drive called “FINAL v3”. Someone puts the client on hold and goes looking.

That’s the financial adviser tech stack in most small practices, and it’s why choosing financial planning software for advisers is really choosing how the pieces fit together. Not one bad tool, but many reasonable ones: a CRM or advice software, document storage, email and calendars, modelling and research tools, e-signing, a client portal, investment platforms, and the spreadsheets that fill the gaps between them.

A client relationship crosses all of them. Enquiries, meetings, file notes, fact finds, SOA requests, approvals, implementation and reviews each start in one place and are needed somewhere else.

What does piecing tools together really cost?

The licence fees are the visible part. The bigger cost sits in the gaps:

  • Re-keying. The same client details typed into three systems, with three chances to get one wrong.
  • Handovers that depend on memory. The next step lives in someone’s head or inbox until they remember to pass it on.
  • Scattered evidence. When a reviewer asks what was agreed and who approved it, the answer has to be reassembled.
  • Prework between the meeting and the advice. Information captured once has to be found, copied and checked again before anyone can use it.
  • More places to secure. Every tool holds its own copy of client data, with its own logins, permissions and terms, and each is one more thing to check and, one day, export.

Before choosing any software, map one client journey. Note where each piece of information starts, who needs it next and what tells them they can proceed. The gaps show up quickly.

Where is financial planning software heading?

The direction is towards one connected system. A file note, a fact find, an SOA request and a review become steps on one record of the relationship, not files in separate places. Handovers are visible, and anyone authorised can see what is waiting and why. Some people call this a practice operating system. The name matters less than the idea: the firm’s work runs through one place.

Two things make it work. First, it has to be built around the firm: your stages, roles, approval points and templates, not a generic template the team bends to fit. Second, it has to be changeable. Firms change their forms, reports and processes every year, and a system that can’t keep up drives people back to spreadsheets.

It’s a direction, not something you buy in one step. Specialist research tools and investment platforms are likely to stay specialist for some time, and each connection to them still needs testing.

Where does AI fit, and where do people decide?

AI can prepare notes, drafts and next steps from the context already on the record. That’s exactly why one system matters: AI working across four disconnected tools has four partial pictures.

Human approval is a gate, not a formality. A clickthrough isn’t advice approval, and advisers keep the strategy and product judgement. Each AI step needs defined inputs, a clear output, a named reviewer and a plan for when information is incomplete. For how this works on one task, see AI meeting notes for financial advisers.

Worked example (fictional)

Priya and Arjun Shah, a fictional couple, want to know if they can retire at 60 and 63. Here is the same journey through a pieced-together stack and through one system. It’s an illustration of the workflow, not a customer result.

The useful difference is the handover. At each step, the next person knows what they’ve received, what to check and what happens next, without ringing anyone.

Why does the change happen in steps?

Few practices rebuild everything at once, and they shouldn’t. Start with the workflow that is most backed up. For many firms that’s the stretch between the first meeting and the SOA; for others it’s reviews that keep slipping. Connect more as it proves itself.

One system still needs owners. Agree who manages access, looks after connections, resolves exceptions and approves changes. If you’re weighing whether to buy, build or shape that system, the build vs buy guide sets out the questions.

What should you ask of software today?

Compare the process, not the product category. A well-set-up CRM may already do more than you think, and a product called a platform may do less. Bring a real workflow, using sample information, and ask the supplier to show you:

  • A complete client journey, including a handover between two people.
  • What happens when a document is missing or the client match is uncertain.
  • What each role can see and change.
  • How an AI-prepared draft is checked, corrected or rejected, and who approved it.
  • How a form or report is changed, by whom, and how long it takes.
  • Which tools it replaces, which it connects to, and which stay separate.
  • Where client data is kept, and who administers access.

When you compare prices, count the tools a system would replace as well as its own fee. The software cost checklist shows how to put them side by side.

Your next step: this week, map one recent client from first meeting to implemented advice. Count the systems they passed through and the times someone copied information between them. That number tells you where to start.

Common questions

Is a practice operating system something we can buy today?

Not as a single finished product. It’s better treated as a direction: firms get there in steps, connecting their most important workflows first and deciding what to keep, connect or replace along the way.

What’s the difference between a CRM and a practice system?

A CRM mainly holds contacts, notes and activities. A practice system also carries the advice work itself, from fact find to approval and review, with an owner on each step and the evidence kept on the record.

Does one system mean replacing every tool we use?

No. Some specialist tools and platforms will stay. Decide what to keep, replace or connect based on your workflows and the connections available, and confirm those decisions before committing.

Can we start with just one workflow?

Yes, and it’s usually the best way. Pick the workflow that holds your advice up most, prove it on real work, then connect the next one.

Where should our client data live?

Somewhere your firm understands and controls. Ask any supplier where records and documents are stored, who administers access, and how you would get your data out if you left.

Will AI make advice decisions in a connected system?

It shouldn’t. AI can prepare notes, drafts and next steps, but advisers keep the strategy and product judgement, and a person should approve AI-prepared work before it is used or sent.
