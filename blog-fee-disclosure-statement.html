Fee Disclosure Statement: What Changed, and What Replaced It

The yearly statement has gone. A written disclosure and the client’s signed consent now carry the weight, so your records need to show both.

The short answer

Is a fee disclosure statement still required?

No. A fee disclosure statement (FDS) was the yearly statement advisers gave clients in an ongoing fee arrangement, showing fees paid and services received. The Delivering Better Financial Outcomes reforms removed it. For arrangements from 10 January 2025, clients instead give signed, dated written consent each year, after a written disclosure of the coming period’s services and fees.

Key takeaways
  • The DBFO Act removed the annual fee disclosure statement; new ongoing fee arrangements follow ASIC’s INFO 286 from 10 January 2025.
  • Arrangements in force on 10 January 2025 moved to the new rules from their first anniversary after that day.
  • Keep the new consent records and the old FDS records: ASIC’s guidance sets at least five years for both.

What was a fee disclosure statement?

An email lands in March. ‘We always get a fee disclosure statement around now. It hasn’t come. Did we miss it?’ It’s a fair question. For years, anyone paying an ongoing advice fee received one every year. Now they don’t, and your team needs one clear answer about why, and what the client gets instead.

A fee disclosure statement (FDS) was a written statement a fee recipient had to give a client under an ongoing fee arrangement. ASIC’s INFO 256 sets out what it had to include, among other things:

  • the amount of each ongoing fee paid in the previous year
  • the amount of each ongoing fee payable in the upcoming year
  • the services the client received, and was entitled to receive, in the previous year
  • the services the client is entitled to receive in the upcoming year
  • how to renew the arrangement

Under those rules, the FDS had to be given no later than 60 days after the arrangement’s anniversary day each year. The client then had a renewal period of 120 days beginning on the anniversary day. If they didn’t renew, the arrangement terminated 30 days after that period ended.

What changed under the Delivering Better Financial Outcomes reforms?

The Treasury Laws Amendment (Delivering Better Financial Outcomes and Other Measures) Act 2024, usually called the DBFO Act, rewrote the ongoing fee rules. ASIC’s DBFO page says it streamlines the renewal and consent requirements, removes the requirement to provide a fee disclosure statement, and adds flexibility in anniversary date timing. Parts of the Act started on 10 July 2024. The ongoing fee arrangement changes apply to new arrangements from 10 January 2025, which ASIC calls the start day.

Put simply, the backward-looking statement has gone. What remains is a forward-looking disclosure and the client’s signed consent. ASIC’s guidance on the new rules is INFO 286, FAQs on ongoing fee arrangements and consents.

A summary, not the law

This is a plain-English summary of ASIC’s pages as they stood when we checked them on 11 November 2026. Read INFO 286 in full and confirm your process with your licensee.

What replaced the FDS in the annual renewal?

Under INFO 286, before a client consents to enter into or renew an arrangement, you disclose in writing who the fee recipient is, why consent is being sought, the longest period the consent could cover, the services, each fee (or a reasonable estimate and how it’s worked out), how often it’s charged, that the client can end the arrangement at any time, and what happens if they don’t consent. ASIC notes that regulations may prescribe other matters, and says none have been made.

The client then consents in writing, signed and dated. It can be given electronically, but not verbally. Renewal consent must fall inside a window that opens 60 days before the arrangement’s anniversary, or an agreed reference day, and closes 150 days after it. What about arrangements that started before 10 January 2025?

ASIC says INFO 286 applies to arrangements entered into on or after 10 January 2025, and to arrangements already in force on that day from their first anniversary after it. Until then, the guidance in INFO 256 generally continued to apply.

INFO 286 gives an example. An arrangement in force on 10 January 2025 has a 15 December anniversary. The former law applied until 15 December 2025, and the clients could renew under the new rules in the 60 days before that anniversary. If compliant consent wasn’t obtained by 14 May 2026, 150 days after it, the arrangement terminated.

For most files the changeover is now behind you. If an arrangement’s move to the new rules isn’t clear from the record, don’t guess. Check it against INFO 286 and the transitional notes on ASIC’s DBFO page, and with your licensee.

Which records should you keep, old and new?

INFO 286 lists records to keep for at least five years, including:

  • each consent to enter into or renew the arrangement
  • client notices to renew, not renew or terminate
  • the information disclosed to the client
  • the termination date and its basis
  • account deduction consents, any withdrawal or variation, and related communications with account providers

ASIC notes that failing to keep these records is a criminal offence. Don’t clear out the old ones either. INFO 256 required records of each FDS, including the date it was given and how, to be kept for at least five years, so statements from the last years of the old rules are still inside that period. File them on the client’s record, where anyone checking the history of an arrangement can see them next to the newer consents. The same habit underpins an audit-ready advice file.

I used to get a fee disclosure statement. What happens now?

If you’re a client reading this: you haven’t missed anything. The law changed, and the yearly fee disclosure statement is no longer required. ASIC’s Moneysmart site explains what happens instead if you pay an ongoing advice fee:

  • you must give written consent each year to being charged it
  • each time you consent, your adviser must give you written details of the services you’ll receive and how often
  • you can end the arrangement at any time

Before you sign, it’s worth asking: when do I next need to consent, and how will you remind me? Are there product fees on top of your fee? What’s the total cost in dollars? If you want to end the arrangement, tell your adviser in writing.

If you’re unhappy with the fees or the service, talk to your adviser first. Their Financial Services Guide explains how to make a complaint. If you’re still not satisfied, you can contact AFCA, the Australian Financial Complaints Authority, a free and independent service.

What does a good answer to that March email look like?

Worked example (fictional)

Aynub Financial is a fee-for-service practice in Kingaroy, Queensland. Priya Shah emails adviser Mara Holloway asking where this year’s fee disclosure statement is. She and Arjun are planning to retire at 60 and 63, and they like to know what they pay for.

The team drafts a reply the same day, and Mara checks it before it goes. It explains that the rules changed, that the Shahs consented in writing at their last renewal after a written disclosure of the year’s services and fees, and when their next consent window opens. A copy of the signed, dated consent is attached from their record.

Priya replies: ‘So what did we actually get last year?’ Because the meetings, actions and documents sit on the same record, the team can list the two reviews and the help with Arjun’s super rollover, each with a date.

Mara then checks the rest of the book. Two arrangements still have their next renewal worked out from the old FDS calendar. Both are corrected to the anniversary, and the window dates are added to the client record.

Checklist: is your firm fully off the FDS?

  1. Retire the FDS template. Remove it from review workflows and task lists, so nobody sends a document describing renewal rules that no longer apply.
  2. Update client-facing wording. Search your welcome pack, engagement letters and website for any promise of an annual fee disclosure statement.
  3. List every arrangement. Note its anniversary or agreed reference day, and the dates its window opens and closes.
  4. Check the disclosure template. Compare it line by line with INFO 286.
  5. Check each consent. Written, signed, dated, given inside the window and filed on the client’s record.
  6. Match deduction consents. The right account holders, accounts and amounts.
  7. Keep the old records. Past FDSs, with the date and method each was given.
  8. Give the team one answer. A short, approved paragraph for clients who ask where their statement went.
  9. Confirm with your licensee. Especially anything that changed during the transition.

Tie each renewal to your review calendar, but track the consent date separately: a review can move, and the window doesn’t move with it.

Common questions

Is ongoing fee consent still needed every year?

Yes. Renewal is tied to the arrangement’s anniversary, or an agreed reference day, and consent must be given in a window from 60 days before to 150 days after it. ASIC’s Moneysmart site tells clients they must give written consent each year.

What is a reference day?

It’s a day the client and fee recipient can agree to use instead of the anniversary, and the renewal window then runs around it. INFO 286 says it must be earlier than the next anniversary and disclosed before consent is given.

Who decides what the consent disclosure must contain?

The content is set by the Corporations Act, as amended by the DBFO Act, and ASIC explains it in INFO 286. ASIC notes regulations may prescribe other matters, and says none have been made.

Did the FDS apply to one-off advice fees?

No. The FDS was part of the ongoing fee arrangement rules. Fees that aren’t ongoing have their own written request or consent rules, which ASIC explains in INFO 287.

What must happen if renewal consent isn’t given in time?

The arrangement terminates and no further fees can be charged under it. INFO 286 says the fee recipient must notify relevant third-party account providers within 10 business days that fees can no longer be deducted.

How serious is it to lose fee records?

ASIC says failing to keep the required ongoing fee records is a criminal offence. Keep consents, disclosures, notices and termination records for at least five years, and check whether your licensee asks for longer.
